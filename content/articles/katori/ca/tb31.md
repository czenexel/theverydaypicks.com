## The science every home cook needs to know

The research that shook me so badly was published in *Environmental Science & Technology* — one of the most respected scientific journals in the world. Researchers at North Dakota State University tested what happens when you chop food on a regular plastic cutting board.

Their findings were jaw-dropping. A single chopping session releases between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles straight into your food. Over a year of regular use, that could mean as many as 79.4 million microplastic particles entering your body — or about 50 grams of pure plastic.

**What are microplastics?** Microplastics are tiny plastic fragments smaller than 5 millimetres. They form when plastic objects break down through wear and mechanical force — like a knife sliding across a cutting board thousands of times. Once ingested, some can accumulate over time in the gut, blood, and organs.

Here's what got me. For years I'd carefully bought "BPA-free" water bottles and food containers, thinking I was protecting my family from plastic. I know I'm not the only one who did that. But BPA was only ever half the story: BPA is a chemical that *leaches* out of plastic, while microplastics are the plastic *itself*, physically shaving off into your food. And a cutting board, unlike a water bottle, gets scraped by a razor-sharp blade hundreds of times a week. All that "BPA-free" effort — and the bigger problem was sitting right on my counter the whole time.

Then, in 2025, researchers at Nanjing University published an even more alarming follow-up study in *Environmental Health Perspectives*. They fed mice food prepared on plastic cutting boards for 12 weeks and watched what happened.

The result? The mice showed significant changes in gut bacteria, altered liver function, and signs of intestinal inflammation. The study concluded that no plastic cutting board should be considered completely "safe."

This isn't some fringe theory, either. *The New York Times* covered it late last year, and the NDSU research has been cited across major medical publications. The science is clear: plastic cutting boards release significant amounts of microplastic into food, and the long-term health implications are concerning.

As a mom who spends an hour a day in the kitchen, this hit me harder than any other health story I've read in years.

## Why switching to wood or glass isn't the answer

{{< shortcode-image-no-sub link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" >}}

My first thought was obvious: just grab a wooden board from Williams Sonoma and call it a day.

But then I actually dug into it — and it turns out every common cutting board material comes with serious problems.

**Wooden boards** are porous. Bacteria, moisture, and food residue seep into the grain and are nearly impossible to fully sanitize. The knife grooves become breeding grounds for nasty pathogens like salmonella and E. coli. And if you don't oil them regularly, they warp, crack, and start to mould — not exactly ideal in humid summer months.

**Bamboo boards** have the same porosity problems, and many are glued together with adhesives that can contain formaldehyde — a known carcinogen.

**Glass boards** may be hygienic — but they absolutely destroy your knives. Every cut on glass dulls the blade fast, which means less control, more force, and constant trips to the knife sharpener. Anyone who's invested in a good set from Wüsthof or Shun knows that pain.

## "Have you looked into titanium?"

{{< shortcode-image-no-sub link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" image_id="2d7d4959-65f7-4b89-90f9-15694091ed00" var="variant0001" >}}

Honestly? I hadn't. Titanium sounded like something for fighter jets, not kitchen countertops.

But Sarah explained that titanium's entire advantage comes down to one thing — a principle I now think about every time I cook. She called it the **Solid-Surface principle**: a titanium board is a single, solid, sealed piece of inert metal. Nothing can soak *into* it, and nothing can flake *off* it. That one property is the reason it beats every other material — and it breaks down into three "nons":

**Non-porous.** Unlike wood or bamboo, there's nowhere for bacteria, moisture, or food residue to hide. Run it under the faucet for a second and it's clean — no scrubbing, no sanitizing, no special care.

**Non-shedding.** It's a pure metal, not a plastic — there are no particles to grind loose. Your food touches nothing but a clean, inert, food-safe surface. Zero microplastics, ever.

**Non-reactive.** Titanium is so biocompatible it's used for medical implants inside the human body. It won't leach, stain, or hold odours — and the part that surprised me most, its surface is actually gentler on knife edges than glass, ceramic, or regular steel.

On top of that, titanium dioxide — the oxide layer that naturally forms on the surface — has been extensively studied for its antibacterial properties, with research published in *Scientific Reports* pointing to activity against common foodborne pathogens like E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus.

Honestly, I felt like I'd been living under a rock. This material checks absolutely every box — and I'd never heard of anyone using it as a cutting board.
