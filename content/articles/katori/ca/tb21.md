{{< epic-h2 >}}Pure titanium — not plastic, not wood, not glass{{< /epic-h2 >}}

Titanium is a single, solid, non-porous metal. That one fact is what separates it from everything else on your counter.

**It's not plastic.** Plastic boards shed microplastics into your food every time a knife crosses them, and the scratches give bacteria somewhere to live.

**It's not wood or bamboo.** Those are porous — moisture, bacteria, and odors soak into the grain, and they need regular oiling just to survive.

**It's not glass or steel.** Those don't absorb anything, but they wreck your knife edge with every cut. Titanium doesn't. If those three things are clear, the rest of this page is worth your time.

{{< epic-h2 >}}What the science actually says{{< /epic-h2 >}}

I'm not going to hand-wave the health part, because that's where most of these claims fall apart. Here's what's actually been published — and where it stops.

A 2023 study from North Dakota State University, published in *Environmental Science & Technology*, measured what comes off a plastic board when you chop: between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles per session, adding up to as much as ~50 grams of plastic a year.

In 2025, researchers at Nanjing University fed mice food prepared on plastic boards for 12 weeks and reported changes in gut bacteria, liver function, and signs of intestinal inflammation. That's animal data — not proof of harm in people — but it was enough that the authors concluded no plastic board should be treated as entirely "safe."

Titanium sidesteps the question instead of answering it. It's a pure, non-porous metal — the same biocompatible material used in medical implants — so there's nothing to shed and nowhere for bacteria to soak in. Its surface oxide layer (titanium dioxide) also showed antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus in *Scientific Reports*. None of this makes a careless kitchen safe. But as a surface, it removes the variable entirely.
