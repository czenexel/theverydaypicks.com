We shop at the local farmers' market most weekends. I cook from scratch nearly every night — proper meals with fresh veggies, quality meat from the butcher, nothing processed if I can help it. My two kids eat better than most adults I know.

So when I came across a university study while scrolling through my phone one evening after the kids had gone to bed, I genuinely felt sick to my stomach.

**The average person swallows up to 50 grams of microplastics from their cutting board every year.**

That's roughly the weight of ten credit cards — tiny invisible plastic particles ground directly into your food every time you chop, dice, or slice. And I'd been doing it on the same plastic board for years, feeding it to my family without a second thought.

I walked into my kitchen that night and picked up my cutting board — the white plastic one from Walmart I'd been using for about 18 months. I ran my finger across it. Deep grooves, scratches everywhere, faint stains from beets that wouldn't come out no matter how hard I scrubbed.

Every single one of those marks was a place where plastic had been shaved off by my knife — and ended up in our dinner.

I threw it in the trash that same night. And then I spent the next two weeks going down a research rabbit hole that completely changed how I think about food safety in my kitchen.

{{< epic-h2 >}}The Science That Every Canadian Cook Needs to Know{{< /epic-h2 >}}

The research that rattled me was published in *Environmental Science & Technology* — one of the most respected scientific journals in the world. Researchers at North Dakota State University tested what happens when you chop food on a standard plastic cutting board.

Their findings were staggering. A single chopping session releases between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles directly onto your food. Over a year of regular use, that could mean up to 79.4 million microplastic particles entering your body — or roughly 50 grams of pure plastic.

**What are microplastics?** Microplastics are tiny fragments of plastic less than 5mm in size. They're created when plastic items break down through wear and mechanical force — like a knife cutting across a cutting board thousands of times. Once you ingest them, some may accumulate in your gut, blood, and organs over time.

Then in 2025, researchers at Nanjing University published an even more alarming follow-up in *Environmental Health Perspectives*. They fed mice food that had been prepared on plastic cutting boards over 12 weeks and tracked what happened.

The results? Mice showed significant changes in gut bacteria, altered liver function, and signs of intestinal inflammation. The study concluded that no plastic cutting board should be considered entirely "safe."

This isn't some fringe theory, either. *Time* Magazine covered the story late last year, and the NDSU research has been cited across major medical publications. The science is clear: plastic cutting boards release significant quantities of microplastics into food, and the long-term health effects are a genuine concern.

As a mom who spends an hour a day at the kitchen counter, this hit me harder than any health news I'd read in years.

{{< epic-h2 >}}Why Switching to Wood or Glass Isn't the Answer{{< /epic-h2 >}}

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="Katori titanium cutting board" >}}

My first thought was obvious: grab a wooden board from Williams Sonoma and call it done.

But then I actually looked into it — and it turns out every common cutting board material has serious problems.

**Wooden boards** are porous. Bacteria, moisture, and food residue seep into the grain and become nearly impossible to fully sanitize. The knife grooves become breeding grounds for nasties like Salmonella and E. coli. And if you don't oil them regularly, they warp, crack, and grow mold — not exactly ideal in the humid summer months.

**Bamboo boards** have the same porosity issues, and many are glued together with adhesives that can contain formaldehyde — a known carcinogen.

**Glass boards** are hygienic, sure — but they absolutely wreck your knives. Every chop on glass rapidly dulls your blade, meaning less control, more force, and constant trips to get them sharpened. Anyone who's invested in a decent set of Victorinox or Global knives knows that pain.

**Titanium is completely non-porous.** Unlike wood or bamboo, there's nowhere for bacteria, moisture, or food particles to hide. A quick rinse under the faucet and it's clean — no scrubbing, no bleach, no special maintenance.

**Gentle on knives.** This is the one that surprised me. Unlike glass, ceramic, or steel — which all destroy your edges — titanium's surface actually helps keep blades sharper for longer.

And titanium dioxide, the oxide layer that naturally forms on the surface, has been extensively studied for antibacterial properties. Research published in *Scientific Reports* has shown it's effective against common foodborne pathogens including E. coli and Staphylococcus aureus.
