{{< epic-lede >}}My son Michael runs samples in a hospital microbiology lab. Last spring I asked him to test my cutting boards, and he told me no.{{< /epic-lede >}}

He's thirty-four and he has been very patient with me for thirty-four years, but he was firm about this one. "Mom, that's not how any of this works. You'd need controls. You'd need replicates. Whatever number I gave you, you'd go and tell people it meant something, and it wouldn't."

He was right, and I asked him again anyway. Three times, over two weeks.

I should explain why I wouldn't let it go. It started with a stranger who refused to touch my cutting board.

For our 35th wedding anniversary this spring, our kids surprised us with a private Japanese dinner, cooked by a real chef in our own kitchen. Chef Hiro arrived with a knife roll worth more than my car payment — and, tucked in beside the knives, his own cutting board.

I laughed and told him we had plenty. He smiled, picked up my white plastic one, and held it up to the light.

"Do you see all these scratches?" he asked, running his thumb along the grooves. "Every one is a place bacteria hide — down where your sponge never reaches. You cut raw chicken here, then vegetables. Where do you think those germs go?"

I just stared at him.

"Onto the next thing you slice," he said, setting it back down. "A scratched board like this, you cannot truly clean. I stopped cutting on plastic years ago. Most chefs I know back home have too."

{{< epic-h2 >}}The number that made me throw my board out that night{{< /epic-h2 >}}

I laughed it off at the table. But after everyone had gone home I got out my iPad, and ten minutes in I found the figure that made my stomach drop — reported by Dr. Charles Gerba, a University of Arizona microbiologist who has spent decades swabbing the surfaces in ordinary homes:

**The average kitchen cutting board can carry up to 200 times more fecal bacteria than a toilet seat.**

Not on the smooth part you can see. Down inside the hundreds of tiny knife cuts you can't.

I put mine in the trash before I went to bed. And then I didn't replace it — because of one thought I couldn't get past.

**That board had looked clean to me.** I'd washed it after every use for eighteen months and I'd have sworn on my grandchildren it was spotless. If I couldn't see the problem at my own sink, I wasn't going to see it in a store aisle either, holding up packages that all say the same three words: *easy to clean.*

Which is when I called Michael. And which is why I kept calling.

{{< epic-h2 >}}"Mom. Does Ellie eat at your house on Sundays?"{{< /epic-h2 >}}

The third time I asked, he didn't say no straight away. He didn't say anything for a few seconds, which is not like him.

Then: "Mom. Does Ellie eat at your house on Sundays?"

Ellie is his daughter. She is four. She sits on the counter while I cook and asks for cucumber slices, and she has done that every Sunday of her life.

He called me back the next morning and said he'd do it. Not because I'd worn him down — he told me that plainly, and I believe him. He'd gone into work and thought about it between samples, and he'd arrived somewhere he didn't like.

But he had conditions, and he listed them before I could say thank you.

**He would design it, not me.** I could buy the boards. That was the extent of my involvement.

**He'd buy the plates himself and run them on his own time.** He said that twice, and he asked me to write it down if I ever wrote any of this anywhere. So there it is.

**Three runs per board, not one.** Anything can happen once.

**Two controls.** A clean dinner plate out of my cupboard, swabbed exactly like the boards — something we both already trusted. And a plate he'd open and close without touching anything, to prove the plates themselves weren't dirty.

**And he wouldn't tell me what the bacteria were.** This was the condition he was most serious about. He said counting how much grows is one job and identifying what it is is a completely different one, and if I ever quoted him saying the words *E. coli* he would never help me with anything again.

{{< epic-h2 >}}The masking tape{{< /epic-h2 >}}

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="c9db25ad-5107-4e7e-e42c-9059de9a0500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

I bought what a normal person actually considers. **Four plastic** — including the same $12 white one I'd thrown away, rebought on purpose, and one advertising itself as antimicrobial. **Three wooden**, including an $89 end-grain walnut block of the kind people get as wedding gifts. **Two bamboo. One tempered glass, one stainless steel.** **And one I'd added on a hunch.** It came to $400 on the nose.

Then I cooked on all twelve for two weeks. Chicken, fish, onions, garlic — real food, rotated so every board took real knife damage. Bacteria were going to be Michael's department; I kept my own score of something he had no interest in, which was what each board was doing to my knives.

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="4959fd97-70e4-4641-45ee-3996c93bdb00" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

Michael came over on a Saturday with a cooler, a box of plates, and a roll of masking tape.

The tape was the part I hadn't expected. He covered every brand name and every label, and he numbered the boards one to twelve in an order he wrote down and didn't show me. When he counted the plates two days later, he would not know which board he was looking at.

I asked him whether that was really necessary, in my own kitchen, with my own son.

He said: "Especially in your own kitchen, with your own son. You already want one of these to win. I could give you that without either of us noticing."

Then he ran it. On every board, the same thing:

- **Raw chicken**, the same amount over the same area. The most ordinary thing that happens in an American kitchen on a Tuesday.
- **Then washed the way I actually wash** — hot water, dish soap, thirty seconds with a sponge. Not bleach, not a sanitizing cycle. What a person does. A fresh sponge each time.
- **Then he swabbed it,** and did something I couldn't have: he diluted each sample down in stages, so that what grew could be counted as individual colonies instead of running together into an unreadable lawn.

Thirty-nine plates went back into the cooler. Twelve boards, three runs each, two controls and a spare.

For the record: before he arrived, I wrote down which board I expected to win. I wrote *walnut.* There's a study people quote in cooking groups about wood being naturally antibacterial, and I'd repeated it myself more than once.

{{< epic-h2 >}}What came back{{< /epic-h2 >}}

He called on Monday evening with the numbers, still reading them off by board number, still not knowing which was which. I had the key in front of me and filled it in as he went.

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="85d521d4-b7c4-406f-3ea0-45b353975a00" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

He got to number four and stopped. "That one," he said. "What is that one?"

It was the $12 white plastic board from the supermarket. The same one I'd been using for eighteen months and would have sworn was clean. **Sixty-eight thousand.**

He said the number twice.

The others weren't much better. The thick restaurant-supply plastic came back at 52,000. The flexible mats at 31,000. The bamboo board with the juice groove at 24,000 — bamboo isn't one piece, it's strips glued together, and the seams give everything somewhere else to go.

**The one that advertised itself as antimicrobial came back at 11,000.** Michael was less surprised by that than I was. A surface treatment protects the surface, he said. The knife grooves are what's left *after* the surface has been cut away. It was the only board that had promised me something, and it's the only one I felt lied to by.

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

**And I was wrong about the walnut.** Forty-seven thousand — worse than the $40 maple at 6,800 and the $32 teak at 4,200, and worse than most of the plastic. The most expensive board in my kitchen, the one I'd picked to win, the one I'd have proudly left out for guests to admire. Wood is porous, Michael said. It doesn't hold bacteria in the grooves so much as let them into the grain, where washing the surface simply doesn't reach.

Then he read the controls. The clean dinner plate from my cupboard: **20.** The plate he'd opened and closed without touching anything: **zero**, which is how we knew the rest of it was real.

{{< epic-h2 >}}The two that passed — and ruined my knives{{< /epic-h2 >}}

Two boards came back almost clean, and I want to be fair about them, because it's the most surprising result in the whole thing.

The stainless steel board came in at **90**. The tempered glass at **130**. Against a cupboard plate at 20, those are not perfect — but they are in a different world from 68,000, and Michael said so without any prompting from me. Two weeks of hard use had barely marked those two surfaces. There was nowhere for anything to hide, and ordinary washing genuinely got it.

On the bacteria question, glass and steel beat every plastic and wooden board in my kitchen by a distance that isn't close.

Then I picked up a tomato.

The knife I'd been using on the glass board wouldn't slice it. It skated across the skin, and when I pushed, it crushed. The steel board was nearly as bad. Every cut on glass is steel against something harder than steel, and the edge loses that argument every single time.

**So every board in that lineup made me choose.** Plastic, wood and bamboo were kind to my knives and impossible to truly clean. Glass and steel came back nearly spotless and destroyed my knives doing it. Eleven boards — and not one of them could give me both.
