{{< epic-lede >}}I've been cooking for my family for over 30 years, and I consider myself pretty careful about what we eat.{{< /epic-lede >}}

Most weekends we shop at the farmers market. I cook from scratch almost every night — real meals with fresh vegetables, good meat from the butcher, and nothing processed if I can help it. I love hosting Sunday dinners for my kids and grandkids, and I'd like to think they eat better than most.

So when our 35th wedding anniversary came around this spring, our kids chipped in and surprised us with something we'd never have booked ourselves: a private Japanese dinner, cooked by a real chef right in our own kitchen.

Chef Hiro arrived with a knife roll that probably cost more than my car payment. But what caught my eye was something else: tucked in next to the knives, he'd brought his own cutting board.

I laughed and told him we owned cutting boards — he really didn't have to pack one. He smiled politely and kept setting up. Later, while he was prepping, I asked him about it.

Instead of answering, he picked up my cutting board — the white plastic one I'd been using for about 18 months — and held it up under the kitchen light.

"Do you see all these scratches?" he asked, running his thumb across the deep grooves and knife marks. "That's plastic your knife shaved off the board. So where do you think it all went?"

I just stared at him.

"It went into the food," he said, setting it back down. "I stopped cutting on plastic years ago. Most chefs I know back home have too."

I laughed it off in the moment — I've been cooking since before this young man was born. But that night, after everyone had gone home, I couldn't stop thinking about it. So I got out my iPad and started reading. Within ten minutes I found a university study that literally made my stomach drop.

**The average person swallows up to 50 grams of microplastics from their cutting board every year.**

That's roughly the weight of ten credit cards — tiny, invisible plastic particles getting ground straight into your food every time you slice, dice, or chop. And I had been doing exactly that on plastic boards just like mine for decades, mindlessly serving it to my husband, my kids, and my grandkids.

I walked back into my kitchen and picked up my board again. I ran my finger across it. Deep grooves, rough fuzz along the cuts, faint beet stains that wouldn't come out no matter how hard I scrubbed. Every single one of those marks was a spot where plastic had been shaved off by my knife — and ended up in our dinner. Exactly like Chef Hiro said.

I threw it in the trash that same night. And then I spent the next two weeks diving so deep into research that it completely changed the way I look at my kitchen.

{{< epic-h2 >}}The science every home cook needs to know{{< /epic-h2 >}}

The research that shook me so badly was published in *Environmental Science & Technology* — one of the most respected scientific journals in the world. Researchers at North Dakota State University tested what happens when you chop food on a regular plastic cutting board.

Their findings were jaw-dropping. A single chopping session releases between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles straight into your food. Over a year of regular use, that adds up to as many as 79.4 million microplastic particles entering your body — or about 50 grams of pure plastic.

**What are microplastics?** They're tiny plastic fragments smaller than 5 millimeters. They form when everyday plastic wears down from friction and pressure — like a sharp knife slicing across a cutting board thousands of times. Once you swallow them, research shows they don't all pass through. Some build up over time in your gut, bloodstream, and organs.

Then, in 2025, researchers at Nanjing University published an even more alarming follow-up study in *Environmental Health Perspectives*. They fed mice food prepared on plastic cutting boards for 12 weeks and watched what happened.

The result? The mice showed disrupted gut bacteria, altered liver function, and clear signs of intestinal inflammation. The study concluded that no plastic cutting board should be considered completely "safe."

**But here is what really worried me.** As we get older, our bodies have a much harder time clearing out everyday toxins. Doctors are now warning that these tiny plastics can mess with your hormones, trigger stubborn gut issues, stir up chronic joint inflammation, and drain your daily energy. It honestly made me wonder if invisible plastic was the hidden culprit behind so many of those nagging digestive problems and aches people my age just shrug off as "getting older."

As someone who spends at least an hour a day cooking for the people I love, this hit me harder than any health story I've read in years. A professional chef had taken one look at my board and known all of it instantly — and I'd had no clue.

{{< epic-h2 >}}Why switching to wood or glass isn't the answer{{< /epic-h2 >}}

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="Cutting board comparison in kitchen" >}}

My first thought was obvious: just buy a heavy wooden board and call it a day.

But then I actually dug into it — and it turns out every common cutting board material comes with serious headaches.

**Wooden boards** are porous like a sponge. Raw meat juices, moisture, and bacteria seep deep into the grain where dish soap can't reach them. Those knife grooves quickly turn into pockets for nasty bugs like salmonella and E. coli. Plus, you can't put them in the dishwasher, and if you don't rub them down with mineral oil every month, they warp, crack, and mildew. Who wants another kitchen chore?

**Bamboo boards** have the exact same germ-trapping grooves, and many are pressed together with industrial glues that contain formaldehyde.

**Glass boards** are honestly terrifying. They're slippery, your knife skates dangerously when cutting wet vegetables, and that awful screeching clack sets my teeth on edge. Worse, glass ruins good knives in days — dulling the blade flat so you have to push harder with less control.
