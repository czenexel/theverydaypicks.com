{{< epic-lede >}}Last spring I knocked on eleven doors on my street with a box of lab plates and a strange request: let me swab your cutting board. Every one of my neighbors swore theirs was clean. So did I.{{< /epic-lede >}}

Eleven of us were wrong. The twelfth board belonged to Kenji, a retired sushi chef two doors down — and he told me, with a perfectly straight face, that he hadn't scrubbed it in six years.

It started at my own kitchen counter, the night we hosted the street's summer potluck.

Kenji had offered to make the sashimi, and he arrived with his knives rolled up in a cloth — and, tucked in beside them, his own cutting board. I laughed and told him we had plenty. He smiled, picked up my white plastic one, and held it up to the light.

"Do you see all these scratches?" he asked, running his thumb along the grooves. "Every one is a place bacteria hide — down where your sponge never reaches. You cut raw chicken here, then vegetables. Where do you think those germs go?"

I told him I wash it after every single use.

"Everybody washes," he said, setting it back down. "Washed is not clean. In my restaurant, the inspector swabbed our boards every few months. Most home boards would not pass. I stopped cutting on plastic thirty years ago."

{{< epic-h2 >}}The number that kept me up that night{{< /epic-h2 >}}

I laughed it off over dessert. But after everyone had gone home I got out my iPad, and ten minutes in I found the figure that made my stomach drop — reported by Dr. Charles Gerba, a University of Arizona microbiologist who has spent decades swabbing the surfaces in ordinary homes:

**The average kitchen cutting board can carry up to 200 times more fecal bacteria than a toilet seat.**

Not on the smooth part you can see. Down inside the hundreds of tiny knife cuts you can't.

My board looked clean. I'd washed it after every use for eighteen months and I'd have sworn on my grandchildren it was spotless. And that was the problem: if I couldn't see it at my own sink, nobody on my street could see it at theirs either.

So I stopped guessing. I decided to find out.

{{< epic-h2 >}}Twelve kitchens, and one very strange favor{{< /epic-h2 >}}

It turns out you can simply buy the answer. The plates food producers use for routine bacteria counts are sold to anyone — fifty for $58. You touch a swab to one, keep it warm for two days, and count what grows. I bought a $40 incubator meant for hatching chicken eggs. My husband did ask, at this point, whether we were going to talk about it.

Then I went door to door. I didn't buy new boards — new boards are always clean. I wanted **the boards people actually use**, at the age they actually are, washed the way they actually wash them.

My rules were simple, and the same in every kitchen:

- **Cook dinner the way you always do** — I only asked that raw chicken, meat or fish touch the board at some point.
- **Wash it the way you always wash it.** No extra effort, no bleach for my benefit. Then leave it in the rack overnight.
- **In the morning, I swab it, plate it, and wait forty-eight hours.**

What came back was a pretty fair picture of America's kitchen drawers: **four plastic boards, three wooden ones, two bamboo, one tempered glass, one stainless steel — and Kenji's.**

And I grew a control alongside them: **a clean dinner plate straight out of my own cupboard.** Something I already trusted. Something I'd happily eat off.

Before the first plate went into the incubator, I wrote down which board I expected to win. I wrote *Carol's walnut* — an $89 end-grain block, the kind people get as wedding gifts, and easily the most beautiful board on the street.

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="85d521d4-b7c4-406f-3ea0-45b353975a00" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

**The four plastic boards were the worst, and it wasn't close.** Three were too many to count — that's an actual term, and it means the colonies ran together into a lawn and a number stopped being meaningful. One of the three was mine. Eighteen months of knife strokes had carved thousands of microscopic canyons, and the dish soap hadn't reached any of them.

**My neighbor Linda's "antimicrobial" board failed with the rest.** 230 colonies, from a board with a silver-ion treatment printed on the label. A surface treatment protects the surface — but the grooves are what's left *after* the surface has been cut away. Linda had paid extra for that promise. She was the only neighbor who got angry.

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="" >}}

**The wood and bamboo were no better — and my prediction was wrong.** Carol's walnut was too many to count, worse than Tom and Judy's maple at 180 and Ruth's teak at 150. The most expensive board on the street had bought her a beautiful object, not a clean surface. Both bamboo boards did the same, their glued seams giving everything one more place to settle.

Nine boards, all still holding something after a wash their owners would have called thorough. And the clean plate from my cupboard came back at **three colonies.**

{{< epic-h2 >}}The two that came back clean — and what they'd done to the knives{{< /epic-h2 >}}

Then I got to Marianne's glass board and Greg's stainless steel one, and for about a day I thought the answer was simple.

**They passed.** Steel came back at 8, glass at 11 — close to a plate from my own cupboard. Nothing had been able to cut into those surfaces, so there was nowhere to hide. On the cleaning question, glass and steel beat every plastic and wooden board on the street by a distance that isn't close.

Then I asked to borrow a knife. Marianne's wouldn't slice a tomato — it skated across the skin, and when I pushed, it crushed. "Oh, I buy a new knife every Christmas," she said. Greg's was nearly as bad. Every cut on glass or steel is a blade against something harder than itself, and the blade loses that argument every time.

**So every board on my street had made its owner choose.** Plastic, wood and bamboo were kind to the knives and impossible to truly clean. Glass and steel came back spotless and ruined the knives. Eleven kitchens — and not one board could give its owner both.
