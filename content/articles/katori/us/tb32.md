{{< epic-lede >}}I consider myself a pretty careful mom when it comes to what my family eats.{{< /epic-lede >}}

Most weekends we shop at the farmers market. I cook from scratch almost every night — real meals with fresh vegetables, good meat from the butcher, and nothing processed if I can help it. My two kids eat better than most adults I know.

So when I was scrolling on my phone one night after the kids were in bed and stumbled on a university study, it literally made me sick to my stomach.

**The average person swallows up to 50 grams of microplastics from their cutting board every year.**

That's roughly the weight of ten credit cards — tiny, invisible plastic particles getting ground straight into your food every time you slice, dice, or chop. And I had been doing exactly that on the same plastic board for years, mindlessly serving it to my family.

I walked into my kitchen that same night and picked up my cutting board — the white plastic one from Target I'd been using for about 18 months. I ran my finger across it. Deep grooves, scratches everywhere, faint beet stains that just wouldn't come out no matter how hard I scrubbed.

Every single one of those marks was a spot where plastic had been shaved off by my knife — and ended up in our dinner.

I threw it in the trash that same night. And then I spent the next two weeks diving so deep into research that it completely changed the way I look at food safety in the kitchen.

{{< epic-h2 >}}The science every home cook needs to know{{< /epic-h2 >}}

The research that shook me so badly was published in *Environmental Science & Technology* — one of the most respected scientific journals in the world. Researchers at North Dakota State University tested what happens when you chop food on a regular plastic cutting board.

Their findings were jaw-dropping. A single chopping session releases between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles straight into your food. Over a year of regular use, that could mean as many as 79.4 million microplastic particles entering your body — or about 50 grams of pure plastic.

**What are microplastics?** Microplastics are tiny plastic fragments smaller than 5 millimeters. They form when plastic objects break down through wear and mechanical force — like a knife sliding across a cutting board thousands of times. Once ingested, some can accumulate over time in the gut, blood, and organs.

Then, in 2025, researchers at Nanjing University published an even more alarming follow-up study in *Environmental Health Perspectives*. They fed mice food prepared on plastic cutting boards for 12 weeks and watched what happened.

The result? The mice showed significant changes in gut bacteria, altered liver function, and signs of intestinal inflammation. The study concluded that no plastic cutting board should be considered completely "safe."

This isn't some fringe theory, either. *The New York Times* covered it late last year, and the NDSU research has been cited across major medical publications. The science is clear: plastic cutting boards release significant amounts of microplastic into food, and the long-term health implications are concerning.

As a mom who spends an hour a day in the kitchen, this hit me harder than any other health story I've read in years.

{{< epic-h2 >}}Why switching to wood or glass isn't the answer{{< /epic-h2 >}}

{{< epic-figure-link image_id="86a60472-8489-44d2-d542-5ee005a0c500" var="variant0001" link="https://track.theverydaypicks.com/click" alt="Katori titanium cutting board" >}}

My first thought was obvious: just grab a wooden board from Williams Sonoma and call it a day.

But then I actually dug into it — and it turns out every common cutting board material comes with serious problems.

**Wooden boards** are porous. Bacteria, moisture, and food residue seep into the grain and are nearly impossible to fully sanitize. The knife grooves become breeding grounds for nasty pathogens like salmonella and E. coli. And if you don't oil them regularly, they warp, crack, and start to mold — not exactly ideal in humid summer months.

**Bamboo boards** have the same porosity problems, and many are glued together with adhesives that can contain formaldehyde — a known carcinogen.

**Glass boards** may be hygienic — but they absolutely destroy your knives. Every cut on glass dulls the blade fast, which means less control, more force, and constant trips to the knife sharpener. Anyone who's invested in a good set from Wüsthof or Shun knows that pain.
