{{< epic-h2 >}}Pure titanium — not plastic, not wood, not glass{{< /epic-h2 >}}

Titanium is a single, solid, non-porous metal. That one fact is what separates it from everything else sitting on your countertop.

**It's not plastic.** Plastic boards shed microplastics into your food with every pass of a knife, and bacteria settle into the knife grooves.

**It's not wood or bamboo.** Both are porous — moisture, bacteria, and odors seep into the grain, and without regular oiling, the boards don't last.

**It's not glass or stainless steel.** Those don't absorb anything, but they wreck your blade with every cut. Titanium doesn't. Once those three points are clear, the rest of this page is worth your time.

{{< epic-h2 >}}What the science actually says{{< /epic-h2 >}}

I'm not glossing over the health part — that's exactly where most of these claims fall apart. Here's what's actually been published, and where it stops.

A 2023 study from North Dakota State University, published in *Environmental Science & Technology*, measured what comes off a plastic board when you cut on it: between 1,536 and 7,680 microplastic particles per cutting session, extrapolated to roughly 50 grams of plastic per year.

In 2025, researchers at Nanjing University fed mice food prepared on plastic boards for twelve weeks and reported changes in gut microbiota, liver function, and signs of intestinal inflammation. That's animal data — not proof of harm in humans — but enough that the authors concluded no plastic board should be considered fully "safe."

Titanium sidesteps the question rather than answering it. It's a pure, non-porous metal — the same biocompatible material used in medical implants — so there's nothing to shed and nowhere for bacteria to nest. The oxide layer on the surface (titanium dioxide) also showed antibacterial activity against E. coli and S. aureus in *Scientific Reports*. None of this makes a sloppy kitchen safe. But as a surface, it simply eliminates the variable.
